The Forecast

The Matterhorn is falling apart

The Matterhorn just shed a chunk of its south face. A massive rockfall tore off from around 3,900 meters Wednesday morning, sending a dust cloud down one of the most recognizable mountains on earth and prompting an immediate suspension of guided climbing on the Italian side. Nobody was caught in it, but the message is clear: permafrost degradation is redrawing the rules of alpine terrain in real time. Meanwhile, the Southern Hemisphere continues to do obscene things. Valle Nevado is still sitting on 414 centimeters — that number hasn't budged because it hasn't needed to; the base is so deep the mountain is basically a glacier with a lift system. Las Leñas jumped to 226cm. New Zealand and Australia remain the awkward cousins at the family reunion, scraping by on thin cover while the Andes hog the spotlight. Up north, 4FRNT just dropped its 2026-27 lineup, trail running is having an identity crisis about its own growth, and a surfer in California bought a tiny airplane because contest results weren't doing it for him anymore. August is a strange month. The mountains are reminding us they don't care about our schedules.

The Edit

Patagonia Films heads to Chile's Cochamó commune for 'Running with Huemuls,' a short film about Fundo Puchegüín — a newly protected area that bridges one of Latin America's largest biological corridors. The huemul, Chile's endangered national animal, is the quiet protagonist here, and Patagonia ambassador Felipe Cancino narrates the significance of seeing one in the wild. It's not a shred film — it's the kind of patient, landscape-first filmmaking that reminds you why the mountains matter beyond the sport.
